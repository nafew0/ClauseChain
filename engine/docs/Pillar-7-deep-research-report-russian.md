# Russian Federation — RDTII 2.1 Pillar 7 Domestic Data Governance Evidence

## Scope, polarity and bottom-line coding

This review treats the Russian Federation as of **16 August 2026** and applies the polarity exactly as specified in the rubric. For **P7-I1** and **P7-I2**, the existence of the relevant framework is evidence for **score 0**. For **P7-I3**, I count only genuine **minimum retention floors** and exclude “no longer than necessary”, deletion deadlines and maximum-retention rules. For **P7-I4**, a binding obligation to appoint the functional equivalent of a DPO is sufficient even without a separate economy-wide GDPR-style DPIA duty. For **P7-I5**, I distinguish ordinary access requiring prior judicial authorisation from disclosure/access powers exercisable by an executive or administrative body without obtaining an independent judicial order first.

On that basis, the provision-level evidence supports the following coding:

| Indicator | Research conclusion | Direction |
|---|---|---|
| **P7-I1** | Russia **has** a comprehensive statutory personal-data framework, with implementing Government and Roskomnadzor instruments. | **0** under the absence-scored question |
| **P7-I2** | Russia **has** a dedicated national cybersecurity/information-security framework: Federal Law No. 187-FZ, the Presidential Information Security Doctrine, Decree No. 250, CII categorisation rules and FSTEC security requirements. The statutory 187-FZ component is CII-focused, but it is not merely a scattered security clause. | **0** under the absence-scored question |
| **P7-I3** | Binding minimum floors exist, including **three years** for communications metadata, **one year** for Internet-organiser metadata, **five years** under AML, accounting and tax laws, plus a six-month communications-content rule. | **1** |
| **P7-I4** | A legal-entity personal-data operator must appoint a person responsible for organising personal-data processing—the Russian statutory DPO-equivalent. | **1** |
| **P7-I5** | Russia has both **non-judicial/own-authority disclosure powers** and **court-gated intrusive powers**. Telecom, Internet-organiser, police, AML and tax provisions permit important disclosures without a prior court order; the CPC separately requires judicial authorisation for specified searches/interception/data-connection measures, subject to an urgent exception. | **1** |

The distinction for I5 is important: a rule compelling an operator to hand user information to an authorised executive agency is not transformed into a court-gated rule merely because more intrusive interception may, under a different provision, require a judicial decision. The table therefore records both sides of the court-order test.

### How I would enter the indicator evidence

## Recent subsidiary-law and amendment check

The personal-data subsidiary “wave” in Russia is materially different from the Thai PDPC notification model mentioned in the research instructions. The official Russian publication portal confirms **Roskomnadzor Order No. 178 of 27 October 2022** on assessing harm to data subjects, **Order No. 179 of 28 October 2022** on confirming destruction of personal data, **Order No. 180 of 28 October 2022** establishing notification forms, and **Order No. 187 of 14 November 2022** governing interaction over the personal-data incident register. citeturn25search0turn26search1turn25search1turn38search1

Order No. 178 is relevant to privacy-risk governance, but I have **not** counted it as the score-determinative “DPIA” provision for I4. Its subject is assessing harm that may be caused to data subjects in case of violation; that is not the same thing as a general ex-ante DPIA obligation for high-risk processing. I4 is nevertheless positively established by the much cleaner **Art. 22.1 DPO-equivalent duty**. citeturn25search0turn38search0

Order No. 179 is deliberately **excluded from P7-I3**. It concerns confirmation of destruction of personal data, and destruction/maximum-retention rules cannot be re-labelled as minimum retention floors. citeturn26search1

Order No. 180 supplies the current Roskomnadzor notification forms for processing, changes in notification information and termination of processing. It is useful supplemental evidence for I1 but does not alter the score once 152-FZ is established. citeturn25search1

## Negative findings and exclusions

- **P7-I1 — no negative finding.** I found a horizontal federal personal-data statute plus Government and Roskomnadzor subordinate instruments. Russia therefore does **not** lack a data-protection framework. citeturn38search0turn12search0turn38search1

- **P7-I2 — no negative finding, with a scope caveat.** I did **not** rely on isolated security duties inside privacy/sectoral statutes to manufacture a cybersecurity framework. The controlling evidence instead consists of the dedicated **187-FZ CII Security Law**, the **Information Security Doctrine**, Decree No. 250, Government CII categorisation rules and FSTEC security requirements. The principal statutory act is CII-specific rather than a universally applicable private-sector cybersecurity act, but the national framework itself is dedicated and institutionalised. citeturn2search1turn28search0turn28search1turn28search2turn28search3

- **P7-I3 — maximum-retention/destruction rules were excluded.** In particular, Roskomnadzor Order No. 179 on confirmation of destruction was **not** counted; neither would a generic requirement to keep personal data “no longer than necessary”. Only provisions with an affirmative duration/floor—three years, one year, six months, “not less than five years”, five years—were reported. citeturn26search1turn41search0turn42search8turn42search3turn45search3

- **P7-I4 — I did not identify an economy-wide GDPR-style ex-ante DPIA obligation sufficiently clear to use as the controlling finding.** Roskomnadzor Order No. 178 is a harm-assessment instrument but is not silently treated as equivalent to a GDPR DPIA. This has no effect on the indicator because the alternative DPO prong is independently met by **152-FZ Art. 22.1(1)**. citeturn25search0turn38search0

- **P7-I5 — a finding of “all access is court-gated” would be incorrect.** The CPC supplies court-gated routes for specified searches and communications-data measures, but separate federal provisions compel disclosure to communications/security/police/tax/AML authorities without making a prior judicial order an element of the disclosure power. Conversely, I have **not** characterised the ordinary CPC residential-search rule as warrantless merely because an emergency exception exists; the ordinary rule is court-gated and the Art. 165(5) route is separately labelled as an emergency pre-authorisation exception. citeturn41search0turn41search1turn44search1turn33search2

- I checked the principal federal sectors most likely to produce rubric-relevant floors or access powers: **personal data, CII/cybersecurity, communications, Internet information services, AML, accounting, taxation, police powers and criminal procedure**. I did not find evidence sufficient to justify reporting a separate economy-wide health-data or employment-data minimum floor in addition to the general tax/accounting/archival architecture.
